Proxy pricing explained: per GB vs per IP, and where you overpay

Two providers can look wildly different on price and cost you exactly the same. The reason is that per-gigabyte and per-IP billing pay for different things — and four costs hide in the gap between them.

What each model actually charges for

Per IP (usually datacenter and ISP proxies): you rent a fixed number of addresses per month. Volume is effectively unlimited, but your concurrency is capped by how many addresses you hold.

Per GB (usually residential and mobile): you rent traffic. Address count is effectively unlimited, but every byte you pull has a price — including the bytes you never read.

Cost 1 — bytes you never use

On per-GB billing you pay for the whole response: HTML, images, fonts, CSS, third-party trackers, analytics beacons. Blocking images, fonts and scripts typically removes the majority of the payload while your parser sees the same data.

This is not a micro-optimisation. On a content-heavy target it can be the difference between a workable budget and a project that looks unaffordable.

Cost 2 — failed requests are billable

A blocked request still consumed bytes. A challenge page still consumed bytes. A pool that fails 40% of the time is not cheap at any headline rate, because you paid twice for every usable result: once for the failure, once for the success.

This is why success rate belongs in the price comparison, not in a separate column.

Cost 3 — retries, and the concurrency you buy to avoid them

Retries multiply traffic. Concurrency to work around slow responses multiplies address usage. Both are invisible on the pricing page and very visible on the invoice.

Cost 4 — engineering time

The cheapest pool that requires custom session handling, manual rotation and constant babysitting is not the cheapest pool. If one provider costs an hour a week more than another, that hour usually costs more than the difference in traffic fees.

Then: cost per successful page = spend ÷ validated results. Compare that figure only. Everything else on a pricing page is an input, not an answer.

Worked comparison (illustrative)

Suppose a datacenter pool at a flat monthly price handles 200 000 pages reliably, and a residential pool billed per GB handles the same 200 000 but fails on 20% of your hard URLs and pulls heavy pages. The datacenter option looks crude; on cost per successful page it often wins by a wide margin — unless the hard 20% is the data you actually needed, in which case neither number is relevant and you need a split setup.

That is the real conclusion for most teams: use cheap addresses for the easy majority and residential only for the minority that requires it. Mixing tiers is usually cheaper than buying the top tier for everything.

Practical order of operations

  1. Instrument your job: attempts, validated results, GB consumed, per target group.
  2. Block images, fonts and third-party scripts; measure the saving before changing providers.
  3. Split targets into "easy" and "defended" using real success rates, not guesses.
  4. Route the easy majority to the cheapest adequate tier; reserve residential for the defended minority.
  5. Re-measure monthly. Pool quality and target defences both drift.

Most providers offer several tiers under one account, which makes this split straightforward instead of requiring multiple contracts — IPRoyal, for instance, sells datacenter, ISP and residential pools side by side, so you can route per target group and see the difference in one dashboard.

Short version

  • Per IP buys capacity; per GB buys traffic. They are not comparable at face value.
  • Four costs hide in the gap: unused bytes, failed requests, retries, engineering time.
  • Compare cost per successful page — nothing else.
  • Cheapest adequate tier for the easy majority, residential for the defended minority.
